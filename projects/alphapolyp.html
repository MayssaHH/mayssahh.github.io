AlphaPolyp

Estimating colorectal polyp segmentation, physical dimensions, and volume from a single colonoscopy image.

Research project at the American University of Beirut · Medical AI / Computer Vision

My contribution: model architecture, training strategy, and evaluation.

Motivation

During colonoscopy, identifying a polyp is only part of the problem. Its physical size and morphology influence clinical decisions, including how the lesion is removed and which instruments are used. AlphaPolyp explores whether a computer-vision model can go beyond segmentation and infer a polyp's 3D morphology directly from a single colonoscopy frame: its volume and three orthogonal dimensions, x, y, and z.

One motivation behind the project was the possibility of providing quantitative sizing information directly during colonoscopy and potentially reducing reliance on additional imaging when precise lesion sizing is required. We did not validate that clinical hypothesis in this project; doing so would require real clinical cases with paired colonoscopy images and an independent measurement reference such as CT or another validated sizing method.

Core idea

Instead of treating segmentation and morphology estimation as separate tasks, we built a multi-task model that predicts both simultaneously. Given a single colonoscopy frame, AlphaPolyp produces a pixel-level segmentation mask, estimated polyp volume in mm³, and estimated physical dimensions x, y, and z in mm.

The segmentation prediction is explicitly reused in the regression pathway: a downsampled predicted mask is concatenated with the final encoder representation before global pooling and regression. This allows the morphology estimator to combine deep visual features with information about the predicted polyp region.

Dataset and 3D supervision

We used Synth-Colon, a synthetic colonoscopy dataset that includes rendered frames together with the underlying colon and polyp geometry. Importantly, all images used for training and quantitative evaluation are synthetic.

The dataset contains directly rendered synthetic images as well as CycleGAN-enhanced synthetic images. The latter are still synthetic scenes, but their appearance is translated toward a more realistic colonoscopy style using CycleGAN. They should therefore be understood as realism-enhanced synthetic data, not as real clinical images.

After cleaning and matching annotations, the final project documentation reports 18,378 usable images, each paired with a segmentation mask and four regression targets: volume and x/y/z dimensions.

Generating morphology labels

The regression labels were derived from the original 3D meshes. A Blender pipeline imports the colon and polyp geometry, computes the boolean intersection corresponding to the part of the polyp inside the colon, calculates the resulting 3D volume, extracts its world-space bounding box, and converts the measurements into mm and mm³.

This gave the regression task physically grounded targets derived from the 3D scene rather than estimates inferred from the 2D mask alone.

Model architecture

AlphaPolyp extends a RAPUNet-style encoder-decoder with a regression branch for 3D morphology estimation. The feature backbone is an ImageNet-pretrained CAFormer-S18. Multi-scale CAFormer representations are injected into the RAPUNet encoder, whose RAPU blocks combine residual and atrous-convolution pathways. The decoder aggregates features across resolutions and produces the segmentation mask.

For morphology estimation, the final encoder representation is concatenated with a downsampled segmentation mask and passed through global average pooling followed by a small MLP: Dense 64 → Dense 32 → four outputs corresponding to volume, x, y, and z.

Training

Training followed a staged strategy. We first optimized the regression component while keeping the segmentation network fixed, then unfroze the full architecture and jointly fine-tuned segmentation and morphology prediction. The final documented setup used AdamW, Dice loss for segmentation, MSE for regression, and equal weighting between the two objectives, with geometric and photometric augmentation.

My work focused primarily on adapting the architecture for joint morphology prediction, iterating on the training strategy, and evaluating the resulting system.

Evaluation

We evaluated segmentation and morphology prediction on held-out synthetic and CycleGAN-enhanced synthetic samples. The results show strong segmentation performance within the synthetic domain, while accurate physical morphology recovery from a single frame remains the harder problem.

Clinical sanity check

Synthetic evaluation alone does not establish real-world measurement accuracy. To obtain an exploratory clinical sanity check, we ran the model on 11 real colonoscopy images not used in training or synthetic validation and asked Dr. Alaa Sharara, Professor of Medicine in Gastroenterology at the American University of Beirut Medical Center, to review the morphology predictions qualitatively.

Qualitative examples

Prototype system

We packaged AlphaPolyp as an end-to-end prototype with two Dockerized services: a Flask web application and a separate model API. A user uploads a colonoscopy image, the frontend forwards it to the model service, and the results page displays the segmentation overlay, estimated volume, x/y/z dimensions, and processing time.

Limitations

Synthetic supervision. The main limitation is that the model was trained on synthetic data. CycleGAN enhancement improves appearance realism but does not remove the domain gap to real clinical images.

No real-world metric ground truth. The 11-image clinical review provided a plausibility check only; no paired real 3D ground-truth measurements were available.

Unvalidated clinical motivation. We could not validate whether the system could reduce the need for additional imaging because that would require real clinical scenarios with paired colonoscopy and independent measurement references.

Single-view ambiguity. Estimating 3D size from one monocular image is sensitive to camera distance, perspective, illumination, lesion orientation, and flat or low-contrast morphology.

Training constraints. The project documentation notes that compute limitations restricted exhaustive full-dataset training under all configurations.

What I would explore next

The next step I would care most about is moving from synthetic plausibility to calibrated real-world measurement: collecting paired colonoscopy and reliable 3D size references, quantifying domain shift, and testing whether multi-view or temporal information from colonoscopy video can reduce the depth and scale ambiguity of single-frame estimation.

Other directions include real-image fine-tuning, domain adaptation, uncertainty estimation, multi-view reconstruction, and prospective clinical validation.
